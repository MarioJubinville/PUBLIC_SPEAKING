# Connecting With the Audience

> *The difference between delivering a speech and sharing an experience.*

Connection is the bridge that carries your message from your mind into the hearts and wills of your listeners. When it is working, people do not feel they are being talked *at* — they feel they are being spoken *to*. It is the felt sense, in the other, of being **seen**, being **safe**, and being **met** — assembled from hundreds of small signals that say, *"I am here with you."*

This document is a practical map for growing in it: what connection is, what it is not, the attitudes that make it possible, the skills that carry it, the habits that sustain it, and the mistakes that kill it.

---

## 1. What Connection Is

### Start with the word itself

*Connect* comes from the Latin *connectus* — "bound together, tied together." Not "impressing on," not "reaching toward." Connection is a state in which you and the room are, for a time, *bound to each other.* Everything else in this document follows from that.

### A working definition

Connection is the audience's felt sense of three things, usually in this order:

1. **"This person is real."** — *Presence.* A human being fully here, not a performance and not a content-delivery machine. Presence is the foundation; without it, everything else is staging.
2. **"This is about me."** — *Relevance.* They can see themselves in the story, the question, the "you."
3. **"I can trust this person with what I'm actually thinking."** — *Safety.* This is what allows a room to *move* — to feel, reconsider, decide.

So: **connection = presence + relevance + trust**, experienced simultaneously by a room full of people. Notice what is *not* on that list — entertainment, eloquence, applause. Those can exist without connection, and connection can exist without them.

### The listener's litmus test

A connected listener can honestly say:

> "I understand what you are saying. You understand something about my life. This matters to me, and I can see how to respond."

Five things must be true at once:

1. **"You understand people like me."** You are speaking to actual people with particular worries, hopes, questions, and responsibilities — not an imaginary audience that exists only to receive your outline.
2. **"I can follow you."** Clarity. People should not have to struggle to find your point or how one idea relates to the next. The heavy lifting of making the message understandable is the speaker's job, not the listener's. A deep message and a difficult-to-follow message are not the same thing.
3. **"I can see why this matters."** You have built the bridge from the text or the big idea to the listener's world — so the message reads as a solution to a problem they actually have, not an announcement of a subject.
4. **"You are speaking with me, not performing at me."** Meaningful eye contact, a conversational manner, genuine concern — preparation put in the service of the listener.
5. **"I know what to do with this."** You have engaged the will: helped people consider a concrete response, not merely understood an idea.

### The three dimensions

Every speaker can check three muscles when preparing:

- **The Heart (emotional resonance).** Find the common ground of human experience — the point where your truth intersects their current struggle, joy, or fear. *Vulnerability* builds a bridge where perfection builds a pedestal: when you share a real failure or doubt, the audience drops its guard. *Empathy* is the ability to anticipate how the room is feeling — and to mean it. And *shared emotion*: if you want the audience to feel the gravity of a tragedy or the joy of a victory, you must feel it first. Your emotion is the signal they use to know how to feel.
- **The Mind (intellectual engagement).** Meet the audience where they are cognitively. Translate big ideas into "kitchen table" language. And constantly answer the unspoken question — *"Why does this matter to me right now?"*
- **The Body (physical and energetic presence).** Connection is often non-verbal: eye contact that locks in, open posture, purposeful movement, and the power of the pause. Much of the deepest connection happens in the silence.

### An organizing frame: Rogers' three conditions

Carl Rogers, the founder of person-centered therapy, identified three conditions that make *any* relational influence work. They turn out to be, almost word for word, the three ingredients of a speaker who connects:

- **Congruence** — the person on the platform is real, and the one delivering the talk is the same one who is in the room.
- **Empathic understanding** — the message is built from what the audience is actually carrying.
- **Unconditional positive regard** — the audience is met with respect, not judgment.

Everything below is elaboration on those three.

### Why story matters

There is a useful bit of neuroscience behind all of this: when someone tells a good story, the brain activity of speaker and listener begins to **synchronize** — the listener's brain starts mirroring the teller's. Story is literally the brain's connection mechanism. That is why preaching is ancient, and why the best sermons were just stories.

## 2. What Connection Is Not

Clear four confusions out of the way first:

1. **Not charisma or performance.** Charisma can create the *illusion* of connection — applause without bond.
2. **Not agreement.** You can connect with someone who disagrees with you, and feel distance from someone who fully agrees. Connection is *coherence*: the felt sense that your inner experience is being accurately tracked by another mind. It is a shared pattern, not a shared opinion.
3. **Not intensity.** A quiet, still, unhurried moment can be the deepest connection of the evening. Emotional intensity should serve the meaning of the message, not become a substitute for it.
4. **Not a positive mood.** You can connect through grief, through hard truth, through a difficult silence. Connection is coherence, not sentiment.

And hold this hierarchy in mind:

- **Entertainment:** "That held my attention."
- **Approval:** "I liked what you said."
- **Connection:** "I understood you, and you addressed something real in my life."

They can overlap, but they are not interchangeable. Connecting does not require watering down a challenging message; it requires communicating it with humanity, common ground, and genuine concern.

### The paradox — the most important part

**You cannot aim at connection directly.** The moment "connecting" becomes the goal, it becomes a performance — and the performance breaks the connection. Connection is a *byproduct*. You aim at the *other*: their question, their pain, their face. And when the aim is genuinely on them, it happens almost involuntarily, for both parties.

From the speaker's side, it is recognizable: you get pulled out of self-monitoring, time distorts, and the words stop being *retrieved* and start being *found*. It feels effortless — which is how you know it is real. Effortlessness *under full attention* is what presence feels like from the inside.

## 3. Attitudes: The Invisible Primer

Attitudes are the invisible part of speaking. The audience cannot see your mindset, but they *feel* it. If your skills are the paint on the canvas, your attitudes are the primer — without them, the paint just peels off.

The order of the work matters: **attitudes come first**, because habits practiced with the wrong attitude become performance, and traits without the right attitude become charisma without bond. Fix the attitude — *"I am here for them, and I am on their side"* — and the habits and traits finally get to do their job.

Skills without the right attitude read as a performer or a manipulator. Attitude without skill is sincere but ineffective. You need both — but the posture of the heart sets the direction the tools point.

### 1. Compassion: "These are people to care for, not an audience to impress."

Think of the person carrying grief, the exhausted parent, the newcomer who feels uncertain, the believer quietly struggling with doubt. Let concern for their good shape how you explain, challenge, and encourage.

There is a massive difference between a speaker who is *charismatic* and one who is *loving*: charisma draws attention to the speaker; love draws attention to the listener. This is the move from **impact to investment** — impact is the "hit" (the big laugh, the loud "amen," the ovation); investment is the "hold" (the quiet change in a person's life three days after the talk is over).

Compassion does not mean avoiding difficult truths. It means speaking them without contempt.

> **Practice:** Before preparing, bring three actual people to mind and pray for them. Ask: *"How can I speak truthfully without being careless with what these people are carrying?"*

### 2. Humility: "I stand under this message with you."

Many speakers fall into the *pedestal complex*: because they hold the microphone, they must hold all the answers. That creates a vertical relationship — superior to inferior — which is the enemy of connection.

Humility is not thinking less of yourself; it is thinking of yourself less. It is the shift from **teacher to guide**: a teacher stands at the front of the room; a guide walks beside the traveler. It is the move from *"You need to do X"* to *"I've struggled with this, and here is what I've discovered."* Preach as someone being taught and corrected by the truth — not someone who has already mastered the Christian life — and include honest acknowledgment of your own struggle when it genuinely helps the message.

The result: when you position yourself as a fellow traveler, the barrier of judgment comes down. The audience no longer feels they are being corrected; they feel they are being helped. They move from defense to openness.

> **Practice:** Examine your use of "you" and "we." Rather than "You keep trying to control everything," try "We can say we trust God while still trying to control every outcome." Use "we" honestly, not mechanically — the aim is solidarity, not a technique for disguising accusation.

### 3. Radical Curiosity: "I may not yet understand your experience."

Connection is impossible if you are only interested in your own message. This is the move from **performance to observation**: a performer wants applause; a curious speaker wants understanding. Enter every engagement as a *student of the audience*.

Ask: Who are they? What keeps them awake at 3 a.m.? What are they ashamed of? What are they longing for? Spend as much time researching the *people* as the *text* — What are the specific pressures of this community? What is the collective grief of this group? What is the secret hope they are afraid to voice? And do not assume you know why someone doubts, misses, disagrees, or seems disengaged.

The result: your content becomes **surgical**. You stop giving generic speeches and start giving specific answers. The audience feels *known* — and there is no faster way to connect than to make someone feel truly understood.

> **Practice:** In conversation, ask *"What makes that difficult for you?"* and listen without immediately preparing a correction. Ask a follow-up question before offering an answer. Carry the same curiosity into preparation: *"What reasonable question might a thoughtful listener ask here?"*

### 4. Sincerity: "I do not need to manufacture a spiritual personality."

Aim for consistency between who you are privately and who you are in the pulpit — congruence between the person presented and the person in the room. You can speak passionately without exaggerating a story, forcing emotion, or imitating another preacher's mannerisms.

Personal disclosure should serve the message, not become a way of seeking reassurance or praise. One honest admission — *"I have struggled with this for ten years"* — does more than ten points of application. But note the boundary: sharing a struggle is connection; *dumping* it is. The test is always: **does this help them, or does it let me feel better about being known?**

> **Practice:** Review an illustration or emotional moment and ask: *"Am I saying this because it serves the message — or because I want people to admire me?"*

### 5. Respect: "You deserve an invitation, not manipulation."

Take listeners' questions, intelligence, and freedom to respond seriously. An invitational approach lets people consider what they hear; it does not force an outward response as proof that the sermon worked.

Respect for the audience's intelligence means assuming the room can sit with difficulty, silence, and nuance — no over-explaining, no over-reassuring. And respect includes the **assumption of goodwill**: the audience is on *your* side, not on the panel. (The reverse — spotlight effect plus mind-reading — is the most common perceptual error speakers make.)

> **Practice:** Replace pressure with a clear invitation: *"Here is what this passage asks us to consider. Where might you need to respond?"* Respect does not require watering down conviction — the distinction is between addressing a belief or action and treating a person with contempt.

### 6. Comfort with Tension: "I can be faithful without controlling the outcome."

Weak speakers fear silence, confusion, or emotional heaviness; they rush to fix the mood with a joke or a quick transition. But the most profound connections usually happen in the **gap** — the moment of tension where the audience is wrestling with a truth.

This is the move from **control to presence**: stay present *with* the temperature of the room, whatever it is. When you say something that hits a nerve and the room goes silent, do not rush to fill it. Stand there. Breathe. Let the tension sit. Be the steady anchor, showing them it is okay to feel the discomfort. By being comfortable with tension, you give the audience permission to be honest about their own — and your speaking transforms from a presentation into a shared experience. You are not just talking *about* the struggle; you are sitting *in* it with them.

Cultivate patience alongside it, with people who need time — including those who disagree. Distinguish two desires: *"I want to help people understand and respond"* and *"I need people to respond visibly so I can feel successful."* Encourage the first. Watch the second carefully.

### 7. Teachability: "What they heard matters — not only what I intended."

Be willing to discover that an explanation was confusing, an illustration excluded someone, or your tone conveyed something you did not intend. Receiving feedback is a skill with an attitude attached: treat it as something to examine, not a verdict you must immediately accept or defeat.

> **Practice:** After speaking, ask *"Where did my explanation become difficult to follow?"* or *"Did anything in my tone get in the way?"* — and thank the person before you explain yourself.

### 8. Presence Over Performance: "My aim is them, not the reception."

Other-focused attention is the single most reliable predictor of felt connection: the aim is on the audience's need, not the speaker's performance. It is the same posture a good therapist, friend, or parent brings.

It means **decoupling self-worth from reception** — being able to be bored, interrupted, or misunderstood without your identity being threatened, which is exactly what lets you be fully present. It means a **giving frame**: the talk is a gift handed over, not a display mounted. It means a **presence orientation**: attention on the next sentence and the next face, not the outcome. And it means holding **connection as byproduct**: aim at the other, and let the connection happen.

> **Practice:** Keep one cue in mind during the talk: *"I am explaining something important to these people"* — not *"I am trying to sound like a great speaker."*

### Attitudes that sabotage

Watch for the hidden costs on your side:

- **Audience-as-judges** — under a microscope when you are actually on the same side as the room.
- **Performance orientation** — the talk becomes an evaluation of the self, so every word is defensive.
- **The connection-as-goal error** — aiming directly at "connecting" turns it into a performance.
- **The applause trap** — reading applause as the metric of success, which selects for performance energy over bond.
- **Perfectionism / fear of error** — produces rushing, over-rehearsal, and rigid delivery; the fear of a stumble costs more than the stumble.
- **Ego protection / proving** — the talk as self-defense ("I know this, and you will see") rather than as service.
- **Entitlement or condescension** — the speaker as bestower who "gives" the audience what they "need"; respect has quietly left the building.
- **Outcome anxiety** — the mind on the verdict (*"will they like it?"*) instead of the present (*"what do they need right now?"*).

## 4. Skills: The Tools of the Trade

Skill is the practice side of attitude. Compassion is *wanting* to understand people; listening is the *skill* you practice to understand them. These are trainable muscles — repetition, review, and honest feedback.

### 1. Listening and asking good questions

Connection begins before the pulpit. Learn to draw people out without immediately correcting, advising, or turning the conversation toward your own experience. Ask things like:

- "What has been weighing on you lately?"
- "What do you find difficult to understand about faith?"
- "Where does believing this become difficult in everyday life?"

A useful conversation exercise:

1. Ask, *"What has been difficult for you this week?"*
2. Follow with, *"What makes that especially difficult?"*
3. Summarize: *"So what you are saying is…"*
4. Ask, *"Have I understood you correctly?"*

Listen for the **question beneath the situation**. Someone describing a work problem might be wrestling with rejection, uncertainty, responsibility, or feeling overlooked. Treat that as something to explore — not something you can confidently diagnose.

### 2. Explaining one idea clearly

Develop the ability to explain a biblical or central idea in ordinary language while preserving its meaning. A deep truth and a vague one are different problems; clarity is a craft.

For each major point, explain it through four questions:

- What does it mean?
- Where do we see it in the passage?
- What might someone misunderstand?
- What would it look like in ordinary life?

For example, instead of stopping at *"We need to extend grace,"* explain: *"When someone admits a mistake, you can take it seriously without using it to humiliate them."* Instead of leaving *"reconciliation"* unexplained, say: *"Reconciliation means restoring a broken relationship — not merely stopping the argument, but making a way for people to come near again."*

The test is not, *"Does this sound impressive?"* — it is *"Can someone hearing it once follow what I mean?"*

### 3. Structuring a message people can follow

Practice making the relationship between your ideas *explicit*; the listener should never have to guess why the next part belongs. A reliable structure for each major section:

1. Name a recognizable question.
2. Show how the passage addresses it.
3. Explain the relevant truth.
4. Illustrate it.
5. Invite an appropriate response.

And practice transitions. Rather than an abrupt "Point two," say: *"We have seen why forgiveness matters. Now we need to face what makes it difficult."*

### 4. Choosing and telling useful stories

Develop both story *selection* and story *delivery*. Two techniques carry the most weight:

- **Sensory detail.** Instead of "I was stressed," say "I was sitting in my car in the parking lot, gripping the steering wheel until my knuckles were white." Sensory detail pulls the audience into the scene.
- **The tension arc.** Introduce conflict and delay the resolution. Connection happens in the "gap" between the problem and the solution.

For practice, tell a true story in about a minute using four beats: **Situation** (what was happening), **Tension** (what became difficult), **Turning point** (what changed), **Connection** (how this illuminates the point). Then remove every detail that does not help, and ask a listener: *"What did that story help you understand?"*

The rule: choose a story because it makes the truth clearer, not merely because it produces a strong reaction. And check illustrations with different listeners beforehand — they can reveal whether it connects the way you expect.

### 5. Vocal dynamics

Voice should carry meaning, not compete with it.

- **Tonal variety.** Avoid the predictable "preacher's cadence." Use whispers for intimacy, volume for passion, and flatness for gravity. Tenderness for suffering, seriousness for warning, warmth for welcome.
- **The strategic pause.** A pause after a big point says, *"I'm giving this to you; now take a moment to see where it fits in your life."* It is where the audience processes the information — and where they feel your confidence. Rushing is the death of resonance.
- **Drop your volume to gather them in.** Lower volume forces the room to lean forward; it is the loudest quiet there is.

Do not confuse intensity with constant volume — variation in pace, tone, and volume gives you more range than delivering everything at the same emotional level. Practice one variable at a time: record the same two-minute section twice, changing only your pace and pauses, and compare which version makes the *meaning* easier to follow — not which sounds more dramatic.

### 6. Non-verbal communication

- **One person, one thought.** The single highest-leverage delivery habit. Pick one face, deliver a full thought to that person (3–5 seconds of eye contact), then move to another face. The room is not an audience; it is a mosaic of dozens of one-on-one conversations you are running at once. (A useful pattern: a "Z" scan across the room, making intentional eye contact with individuals — so the person in the back corner feels seen, too.)
- **Open, authentic posture.** Use your hands and movement to emphasize points, not as nervous habits. Open posture signals trust and invitation.
- **Purposeful movement.** Stop pacing nervously. Move to a specific spot to start a new point and stay there — use your physical position to signal a shift in the message.
- **Stay out from behind the barrier.** The lectern is a physical metaphor for a relational wall. If you stand behind it for forty minutes, the message is "I am protected from you."

### 7. Reading the room and adapting

True connection is a dialogue, even in a monologue. Develop "environmental awareness": a glance across the congregation tells you more than a timer. Are they leaning in? Press the point. Drifting? Change tone, energy, or move your body. Confused? Slow down. A glance across the room tells you more than the clock — respond to *them*, not to your script.

Also use the **echo technique**: borrow the language your audience uses. Matching their vocabulary and metaphors creates an instant, subconscious bond.

### 8. Connecting truth to a specific situation

Learn to move from *"This is true"* to *"Here is where this truth meets life."* Compare:

> "We should be patient."

With:

> "When the conversation becomes tense this week, pause before answering. Ask a clarifying question before assuming you know the other person's motives."

For each application, ask:

- In what situation might this matter?
- What makes responding difficult?
- What could a faithful response look like?
- How does that response arise from this passage?

Make applications concrete rather than leaving the listener to work out what you intended. Instead of ending with *"We all need to trust God more,"* try: *"What is one concern you keep trying to control? Name it honestly in prayer, and consider what a faithful next step would look like this week."* And keep examples varied, so application does not always assume the same life circumstances.

A related ministry principle: separate connection from pressure. Give people room to think, question, pray, and respond honestly — do not make a visible emotional reaction your measure of whether you served them well.

### 9. Receiving and using feedback

Develop the skill of asking focused questions, listening without defending yourself, and deciding what to change. Generic praise gives less useful information than specific descriptions of what helped or hindered understanding.

Ask two trusted listeners:

- "What was the central message?"
- "Where did you lose the thread?"
- "Which example clarified something?"
- "What response did you understand me to be inviting?"

Look for **patterns** across answers rather than rebuilding your preaching around one person's preference — and make sure at least one of your observers is not a person who simply agrees with everything.

## 5. Habits and Routines

### Before you speak

- **Build from their question, not your outline.** Shift from "what do I want to say" to "what are they actually carrying?" The message should answer the question they didn't know they had out loud.
- **Story before argument.** Every major point should ride on a concrete story — yours, a listener's, a character's. Stories are how you smuggle a truth past a defensive mind.
- **Include yourself before you instruct.** Earn the right to say "you" by first showing a real "I."
- **Start with a recognizable question or experience.** Instead of announcing the subject, give listeners a doorway: *"Today I will speak about forgiveness"* versus *"Have you ever replayed an argument in your mind — thinking of what you should have said — even though the other person has probably forgotten it?"* Use the experience as the doorway, then move into the passage and let it shape the answer.
- **Make the message easy to follow.** Write the central message in one sentence. Explain every theological term a newcomer might not know. Give each major section a clear connection to the center. Read it aloud and rewrite sentences that sound like an essay rather than speech.
- **Rehearse by speaking, not by memorizing.** The moment a memorized speech derails, presence dies with it. Know the *shape* — three anchors, a story, a point — and speak in your own words each time.
- **Picture specific listeners.** Someone new to faith, someone exhausted, someone grieving, someone who has heard sermons for decades. Ask: *What will each person need explained? Where might each struggle with this message?*

A useful four-sentence preparation exercise:

1. "The central truth I want to communicate is \_\_\_\_\_."
2. "My listeners may find this difficult because \_\_\_\_\_."
3. "An experience that will help them understand it is \_\_\_\_\_."
4. "One meaningful response they could make is \_\_\_\_\_."

### In the moment

- **Open slowly.** The first thirty seconds are almost always too fast from nerves. Stand, let the room land, take the silent beat, and begin slower than feels natural. That opening silence is one of the oldest preacher's tools in the book, for a reason.
- **One person, one thought.** Full thought to one face, then move.
- **Pause where it matters.** The beat before a key line; the silence after it. Let an important question sit before you answer it.
- **Drop your volume to gather the room in.**
- **Stay out from behind the barrier.**
- **Make it "you" speech.** Application lines should point at their Tuesday morning, not at a generalized "people these days."
- **Read the room and shift.** Energy up, story in, pace change — respond to them, not to the clock.
- **Do not apologize.** "Sorry this is long," "I'm a little nervous," "I'll be brief" — apologies cede authority and announce that your focus is on your performance.

### After you speak — the most underused connection window

- **The thirty minutes after the talk are worth more than the last ten minutes of it.** People connect with you then — in the handshake, the "thank you," the quiet *"that was heavy, wasn't it?"* Stay. Greet. Be available. A lot of the actual ministry happens in that post-moment, not on the platform — so do not pour the still-warm connection out the exit door.
- **Record yourself weekly** and review one thing only: where did the eyes go, where did the pauses land, where did I read.
- **Get one honest observer** who will tell you your top leak, not hand you a list of ten.

A manageable weekly routine:

| Stage | Practice |
| :-- | :-- |
| Before preparing | Have one intentional listening conversation; bring three actual people to mind in prayer. |
| While preparing | Write the main message in one sentence and the listener's central question. |
| Before speaking | Record a five-minute section; review clarity, pace, and eye contact. |
| After speaking | Ask two trusted people specific questions — and thank them first. |
| Next time | Work on **one** improvement, not everything at once. |

## 6. Mistakes to Avoid

In roughly order of damage:

1. **Performing *at* instead of *with*.** The instant you are monitoring how you are being received, you have left the room and the connection is gone. The audience is on your side, not on the panel — and they can feel which side you think they are on.
2. **Scan-and-skip eye contact.** Eyes sweeping the room like a lighthouse without ever *landing* reads as nervous, and worse, as "I am not here with any of you."
3. **The info-dump.** No story, no pause, no "you" — just content at speed. The room switches to background mode around minute six.
4. **Apologizing.** "Sorry this is long," "I'm a little nervous," "I'll be brief."
5. **The all-"you" message.** A lecture where the speaker never once shows their own face. No "I" means no handhold; the audience has nothing to connect *to*. You cannot extract a depth you have not modeled.
6. **Entertainment as a substitute for bond.** Jokes, one-liners, performance energy — applause without trust. The cheapest and most common mistake a speaker makes, precisely because it *feels* like it is working in the room.
7. **Rushing the opening.**
8. **The abrupt exit.** Vanishing the moment the "amen" is done.
9. **Filler hedge words.** "Um," "right?", "you know" — each one a tiny flag that says *I'm thinking about my delivery*, which yanks the audience out of the message and into the performance.
10. **Over-vulnerability that serves you, not them.** Sharing a struggle is connection; *dumping* it is.

## 7. How You'll Know It Happened

The indicators of connection, at three speeds:

- **In the room:** leaning in, hushed silence, no phone-checking, collective breathing.
- **Right after:** they can quote you; they repeat a line to a friend; they come to you afterward.
- **The slow ones:** a decision gets made; someone tells you weeks later, *"That was the first time anyone said that to me."* That is the residue of connection — it outlasts the event.

For a preacher, the deepest test: did the room feel *with* you, not *at* you? Did the platform feel like a place of standing together, rather than a place of performance?

## 8. The Diagnostic — and Where to Begin

### When a talk felt "off"

Polished but cold, energetic but empty? Run it through Rogers' three conditions:

1. **Was I congruent?** Was the person delivering the talk the same real person in the room?
2. **Was there empathic understanding?** Was the message built from what *they* carry?
3. **Was there positive regard?** Did they feel respected, not managed?

One of the three is always the one that leaked.

### Three questions before every sermon

1. "Do I want to help these people more than I want to impress them?"
2. "Have I listened enough to understand something of their lives?"
3. "Am I willing to be challenged by this message myself?"

A prayer you could use:

> "Lord, help me love these people, listen without assuming, speak without pretending, and challenge without contempt. Make me willing to learn, and help me leave the results with you."

### A simple growth plan

For each engagement, pick **one attitude** and **one skill** to focus on — not a list of ten.

> *Example: "For this Sunday, my attitude will be the fellow traveler (humility), and my skill focus will be the strategic pause."*

For your next three sermons, a manageable starting routine:

1. Explain the main message aloud in two minutes without reading.
2. Give one ordinary-life example for each major point.
3. Ask two listeners what they understood and where they became confused.

That is not a formula for a powerful experience. It trains you to measure connection by understanding and meaningful engagement — not only by how confident you felt or how visibly the audience reacted.

### The central shift

Everything here reduces to one re-orientation:

> From *"How well am I delivering my material?"* to *"How well am I helping these people receive and understand this message?"*

That audience-centered posture is the whole of the work. Attitudes set the posture, skills build the bridge, habits make it reliable — and when your aim is genuinely on them, the connection happens almost involuntarily, for both parties.

**In one line:** connection is the felt sense, in the other, of being *seen*, being *safe*, and being *met* — assembled from hundreds of small signals that say, *"I am here with you."*
